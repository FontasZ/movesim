How it works

Think of a skeleton lying on the floor, under gravity, with its joints held together the way ligaments and tissue hold them. There are no muscles. You move one part on purpose, such as the knees or the head, and the physics works out where everything else goes.

A little muscle tone is added only to hold each starting position, because no one lies with the knees standing, or sits upright, without it.

Where the numbers come from

Wherever we could, we took the numbers from published research on real people:

  • The bones and their proportions come from CT scans (the BodyParts3D database), and the weight of each body part from standard tables.
  • How far each vertebra can move comes from the classic spine reference by White and Panjabi, refined with later studies.
  • How the spine resists: loose near rest, and stiffer the further it goes. This follows a review of measurements of the lower back in living people.
  • The neck and the hips follow measurements in healthy adults.

A few things are our best estimates, because we found no good measurements: the upper back, the knees, ankles and shoulders, the softness of the body against the floor, and the amount of tone that holds each position.

How we check it

After every change, the body is compared with measurements of living people, for example how much each lower-back vertebra turns when the trunk is rotated. All 15 checks currently fall within the published ranges. That tells us these parts behave plausibly; it does not prove that the whole body moves like a person.

What it cannot show

  • Muscles and habits, effort and ease: the very things Feldenkrais work changes most.
  • You. It is one reference body, and people differ a lot.
  • Breathing, and moving from one position to another, like rolling over or coming to sit.

It is a tool for looking and explaining, not medical advice.
