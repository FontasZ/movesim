The science behind the body

What the simulated body is made of, where each of its numbers comes from, and how well it agrees with measurements of living people.

This is a model, not a person. It shows how movement can travel through a reference skeleton under gravity. It does not show how any particular person moves, what anyone feels, or what is good or safe for anyone. It is not medical advice.

What it is

A physics simulation of an adult skeleton lying, sitting or side-lying on a floor or a chair, built with the MuJoCo physics engine [1]. The body is 44 rigid segments joined by 118 joints: all 24 vertebrae and the skull, a pair of ribs on every thoracic vertebra, the shoulder girdle and arms, the pelvis, legs, feet and toes. It weighs about 70 kg and is 1.76 m tall.

There are no muscles. Between the bones, joint limits and passive tissue (ligaments, capsules, discs and resting muscle) resist movement as measured in living people. A small amount of muscle tone holds each starting position, because no one lies with the knees standing, or sits upright, without it; that tone is kept separate and as small as will hold the position.

A movement takes hold of one part on purpose, like an intention leading the part: a point of the body is guided along a path by a soft spring (the green dots in the player), or a joint is turned toward an angle. Everything else is passive: gravity, the floor, the joints and the tissue decide where the rest of the body goes. That passive response is what the simulation is for.

How the spine resists

Living spines are loose around rest and stiffen steadily toward the end of their range. The meta-analysis of Watt, Callaway and Williams [7] gives the stiffness of the whole lumbar spine in each quarter of its range: it roughly doubles from one quarter to the next. In the model each spinal level resists a bend d from rest with a moment c·(ea·d/r − 1), where r is that level's range; c and a are fitted to the published quartiles, and the levels of a region share c, so in series they give way in proportion to their range.

How well it agrees with measurements

These checks run with every change to the body (movesim validate). Each compares the model with a measurement of living people. The studies differ in subjects, positions and methods, so a pass means that part behaves within the published range; it does not prove the whole body.

What it cannot tell you

  • No muscles, so no habits, holding patterns, effort or ease: the parts that Feldenkrais work changes most. Tone is a single, minimal value per position.
  • One reference body. People differ widely in proportions, mobility and stiffness.
  • The thoracic spine, the shoulders and the knees and ankles rest on assumptions, not measurements.
  • Discs do not compress, the ribcage does not breathe, the shoulder blade hangs from the collarbone instead of gliding over the ribs.
  • Arms meet the legs but can still pass through the trunk and through each other.
  • It cannot yet move from one position to another (rolling over, coming to sit).
