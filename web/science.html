How it works

A physics simulation (MuJoCo [1]) of an adult skeleton, 1.76 m and 70 kg. There are no muscles: joint limits and passive tissue resist movement, and a small amount of tone holds each starting position. A movement leads one part on purpose; gravity, the floor and the joints decide where the rest of the body goes.

Where the numbers come from

  • Bones and proportions: a CT-scanned skeleton (BodyParts3D [2]); masses from de Leva [3].
  • Range of each spinal level: White & Panjabi [4], with thoracic twist from Wilke et al. [5] and the lowest lumbar level from Fujii et al. [6].
  • How the spine resists: loose near rest, stiffening steadily toward the end of the range, fitted to an in-vivo meta-analysis of the lumbar spine (Watt et al. [7]).
  • Neck and hips: passive stiffness measured in living people (De Boe et al. [8]; Leite et al. [9] and a Manual Therapy study [10]). Ankle axes after Inman [11].
  • Assumed, not measured: the thoracic spine resists like the lumbar spine; knees, ankles, shoulders and arms; the soft tissue at the floor; the tone that holds each position.
The validation checks

Run with every change (movesim validate). The studies differ in subjects and methods, so a pass means that part behaves within the published range, not that the whole body is proven. Hover a row for its source.

What it cannot show

  • Muscles, habits, effort and ease, which are what Feldenkrais work changes most.
  • Individual differences: it is one reference body.
  • Breathing, compressing discs, the shoulder blade gliding on the ribs, and moving from one position to another.
